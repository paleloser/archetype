# Editorial & Content Writing Guide

The standard every post, article and landing page is held to. The rules are product-agnostic;
the **examples** come from a bike-maintenance product (a workshop and its cyclists), written as
"Pedalo", to keep them concrete. `/product-setup` and `/cm` replace them with examples from your
product as you approve posts; until then, read them as illustrations of the rule, not as copy.

Below, "the product" is the one in `PRODUCT.md`, and its audiences are the ones listed there.

## 1. The golden rule: sell the problem, not the feature

Our content should not read like product documentation.

**Avoid:**

> Pedalo connects to Strava, analyses kilometres and calculates component wear.

**Prefer:**

> Pedalo helps workshops anticipate maintenance before a bike needs attention.

The feature explains **how** we do it. The benefit explains **why it matters**.

### Rule

Before writing any piece of content, answer:

> **What problem are we solving, and what changes for the user because of the product?**

If the answer isn't obvious from the content, rewrite it.

---

# 2. One main idea per piece of content

Every post or article should have **one central idea**.

Don't try to explain everything the product does in every piece of content.

Examples:

* “Bike maintenance shouldn't depend on someone remembering to visit the workshop.”
* “Your workshop can know when a bike needs maintenance before the cyclist does.”
* “The kilometres your customers ride can become useful information for your workshop.”
* “A workshop can look after its customers' bikes even between visits.”

Everything else should support that central idea.

### Rule

If you can remove a paragraph without weakening the main message, it probably doesn't belong there.

---

# 3. Start with a hook, not an explanation

The opening should make someone want to keep reading.

Good hooks can be:

* A strong statement
* A question
* A familiar problem
* An observation about the industry
* An unexpected idea

**Weak:**

> Today we'd like to introduce our new maintenance platform.

**Better:**

> Bike maintenance shouldn't depend on someone remembering to take their bike to the workshop.

**Also good:**

> What if your workshop knew when your bike needed maintenance before you did?

### Rule

Don't start by explaining what the product is.

Start by explaining **why the reader should care**.

---

# 4. Write like a person, not a corporation

We want to sound professional without sounding corporate.

**Avoid:**

> Our innovative solution enables workshops to optimise and digitise preventive maintenance management.

**Prefer:**

> Pedalo helps workshops anticipate their customers' maintenance needs.

Write like someone who understands cycling and is explaining something useful to another person in the industry.

### Our tone

* Clear
* Direct
* Approachable
* Confident
* Professional
* Optimistic
* Human
* Slightly informal when appropriate

### Avoid corporate language such as

* “comprehensive solution”
* “ecosystem”
* “revolutionary”
* “disruptive”
* “leverage”
* “synergies”
* “digital transformation”
* “next-generation platform”

If a sentence sounds like it belongs in a consulting firm's presentation, it probably isn't our voice.

---

# 5. Use concrete language

Replace abstract concepts with concrete actions whenever possible.

**Abstract:**

> Improve maintenance management.

**Concrete:**

> Know when a bike is approaching its next service.

**Abstract:**

> Facilitate the relationship between workshops and cyclists.

**Concrete:**

> Let workshops know when it's time to contact each cyclist.

### Rule

Ask:

> **“Can I picture this actually happening?”**

If not, make it more concrete.

---

# 6. Explain the benefit before the mechanism

A useful structure is:

**Problem → Benefit → How it works → Action**

For example:

> Bike maintenance shouldn't depend on a cyclist remembering to visit the workshop.
>
> Pedalo helps workshops anticipate maintenance and contact cyclists when their bike is approaching its next service.
>
> It does this by synchronising their rides and estimating component wear.
>
> Create your account and start looking after your customers' bikes.

This structure works particularly well for technology products.

---

# 7. When there are two audiences, speak directly to each one

When the product serves several audiences (`PRODUCT.md` → Audiences), for example:

**Workshop** → wants to manage and look after its customer base more effectively.

**Cyclist** → wants to keep their bike properly maintained without having to remember everything themselves.

Don't mix their benefits.

A useful structure, with each audience's marker from `PRODUCT.md`, is:

> 💼 **If you run a workshop:** ...
>
> 🚴 **If you're a cyclist:** ...

This lets readers quickly identify what's relevant to them.

---

# 8. Don't just describe what the product does — explain what changes

This is one of the most important rules.

Don't write:

> Pedalo monitors kilometres.

Write:

> Pedalo monitors kilometres so your workshop knows when a bike is approaching its next service.

The second sentence connects:

**Feature → Use case → Outcome**

That's the pattern we want throughout our content.

---

# 9. Prefer action verbs

Action verbs make the product feel tangible.

Prefer:

* connect
* sync
* monitor
* estimate
* detect
* notify
* anticipate
* maintain
* review
* contact
* track

Over:

* enable
* facilitate
* provide
* support
* offer
* contribute to

The latter aren't forbidden, but they should not dominate our writing.

---

# 10. Keep sentences and paragraphs short

Especially for LinkedIn.

A good practical rule:

* One idea per paragraph
* 1–3 sentences per paragraph
* Generally keep sentences below 20–25 words
* Use plenty of whitespace

People should be able to scan the content quickly.

If a paragraph looks like a wall of text, break it up.

---

# 11. Don't use complicated words to sound smarter

Clarity is a feature.

**Worse:**

> We determine proximity to the next maintenance cycle through analysis of usage patterns.

**Better:**

> We analyse how each bike is used to know when its next service is approaching.

If we can say something in ten words instead of twenty, we usually should.

---

# 12. Use industry terminology carefully

Industry terminology can make the brand feel authentic. In the example product:

* kilometres
* wear
* maintenance
* components
* workshop
* cyclist
* ride
* service
* drivetrain

But don't assume everyone understands technical terminology.

The rule is:

> **Write like someone from the industry, but write for everyone.**

---

# 13. Personality is good; aggression isn't

We can use informal language when it fits the brand.

For example:

> Fewer unexpected problems halfway through a ride.

This has personality.

But avoid language that blames the cyclist:

> Stop being careless and take your bike to the workshop.

The brand should communicate that **the product helps**, not that the user is doing something wrong.

---

# 14. Talk about real outcomes, not grand promises

**Avoid:**

> We're revolutionising bike maintenance forever.

**Prefer:**

> Fewer unexpected breakdowns. Less maintenance done too late. More bikes ready to ride.

Specific outcomes are more credible than grand claims.

---

# 15. Use groups of three to reinforce an idea

Three short statements work particularly well for conclusions and headlines:

> Fewer unexpected problems.
> Less maintenance done too late.
> More bikes ready to ride.

Or:

> Connect. Monitor. Anticipate.

Don't overuse this technique, but it's useful when you want a memorable ending.

---

# 16. Make the CTA explicit

Avoid:

> Discover our solution.

Prefer:

> Discover Pedalo.

Or:

> Create your account.

Or:

> Ask your workshop about Pedalo.

The reader should immediately understand what to do next.

---

# 17. End with an action, not another explanation

The final paragraph should answer:

> **“What should I do now?”**

Examples:

**Workshop acquisition**

> Create your account and start looking after your customers' bikes.

**Cyclist acquisition**

> Ask your workshop if they work with Pedalo.

**Educational content**

> Discover how preventive bike maintenance works with Pedalo.

---

# 18. Put technical features after the concept

When introducing technology — Strava, APIs, algorithms, synchronisation, etc. — explain what it enables first.

**Don't write:**

> We use the Strava API to synchronise activities.

**Write:**

> Pedalo automatically synchronises your cyclists' rides with Strava, so your workshop can understand how each bike is actually being used.

Technology is the mechanism, not the message.

---

# 19. Final editorial checklist

Before publishing, answer “yes” to these questions.

### Message

* Is there one clear central idea?
* Is the problem clear?
* Is the benefit clear?
* Can someone understand the value without knowing the product beforehand?

### Writing

* Can we remove any sentence without losing meaning?
* Are any sentences unnecessarily long?
* Are there corporate or overly complicated words?
* Is the content easy to scan?

### Product

* Do we explain the benefit before the technology?
* Have we clearly separated each audience's benefits where appropriate?
* Are all claims accurate?
* Are we avoiding promises the product cannot currently deliver?

### Brand

* Does it sound human rather than corporate?
* Does it have personality?
* Is it professional without being cold?
* Does it avoid exaggerated claims?
* Does it sound like us rather than a generic SaaS company?

### Conversion

* Is the CTA clear?
* Does the reader know what to do next?
* Does the content naturally lead towards the product?

---

# 20. The reference formula

When in doubt, use this structure:

### HOOK

A thought that makes the reader stop.

↓

### PROBLEM

What is happening today, and why does it matter?

↓

### BENEFIT

What changes because of the product?

↓

### HOW

A short explanation of the feature that makes it possible.

↓

### USE CASE

How does each audience actually use it?

↓

### CLOSING

A memorable statement reinforcing the benefit.

↓

### CTA

One clear action.

---

# The rule that summarises everything

> **We don't write to tell people what the product does. We write to make them understand why it matters.**

If, after reading a piece of content, someone can explain in one sentence **what problem the product solves, who it solves it for, and what changes as a result**, the content has done its job.

---

## Language note

These guidelines are written in English for internal use. Customer-facing content is written in
the social media locale from `PRODUCT.md`, with its form of address.

Do not translate the English examples literally. The principles, structure and tone should be
preserved, but the copy should sound like something a native speaker would naturally write. When
choosing between a literal translation and natural language, **always choose natural language**.

## Brand name

Write the brand exactly as `PRODUCT.md` → Brand casing says, everywhere: at the start of a
sentence, in a heading, in a hashtag.

## Product-specific rules

Rules only this product has (words to avoid, claims never to make, how to talk about price) live
in `PRODUCT.md` → Publishing rules, and corrections from past runs in `learnings.md`. Both win
over this guide.
